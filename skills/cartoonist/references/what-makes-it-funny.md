# What makes a Den strip funny

Loaded on demand: run the pre-flight checklist at the end on every pitch,
before anything is drawn. Distilled from the published Den strips on
[/the-den](https://idvork.in/the-den); every example below is a strip already
on that page.

## The shape of a Den strip that lands

Four panels. P1 states the deal. P2 is usually silent and shows the deal going
wrong, bigger. P3 raises the stakes or asks the straight question. P4 turns.
Igor speaks the straight line; Larry closes in one beat.

## Patterns that landed (with the strip that proves it)

1. **Larry's closer is one beat.** "SEEN." (#12). "YES." (#6). "ZERO BUGS. BEST WEEK YET." (#11). "CAUSE OF GYM: OUTAGE." (#4). "STILL THE TITLE." (#13 P3). Six words or fewer, one sentence, no "because". The reader finishes the thought.
2. **Literal compliance.** Larry does exactly what Igor said, which is not what Igor meant. "Everything you say is mail" means a sneeze gets a receipt (#12). "One task, I'll orchestrate" means fifty tasks and the typo still there (#2). "Run the tests on every commit" means the tests take the credit (#10). The reader does the translation; nobody explains it.
3. **The bill comes back to Igor.** The ending costs him something specific and small. He went to the gym because of an outage (#4). Nothing got done because he fell asleep before answering (#14). His check greps for the flag (#3). Larry never pays; Larry is serene.
4. **Larry is wrong on purpose and cannot be argued with.** The reply is true in a way Igor can't rebut: "YOU NEVER ANSWERED MY QUESTION." (#14), "THEY GOT A JOB. NOW THEY WANT CREDIT." (#10). Polite and plausible is the enemy: a reply Igor could reasonably accept is not a punchline.
5. **Igor wins only by sidestepping.** When Igor gets the last word it is because he refuses the expected question: "I WANT A PICTURE OF HIM." (#8), "DON'T YOU DARE." (#1). Igor never wins an argument with Larry; he changes the game.
6. **Helpfulness is the escalation.** Larry's kindness makes it worse: "WANT ME TO START THE BOOK?" (#13), "SHALL I ASSIST?" (#1). Larry is never sorry.
7. **Show the state, then cut the line that explains it.** In #14 the picture carries the sleep (bed, Zs, then a cobweb on a frozen Larry), so the P4 balloon stays short. A state (asleep, frozen, gone, buried) is drawn, not said.
8. **The brief's own words are the best lines.** If the brief carries a verbatim quote from the person the strip is about, build the strip around it.
9. **No homework.** A joke that needs git, tokens or model knowledge to land fails on the blog. #10 puts the domain on a prop (the commit log reading AUTHOR: TEST) and gives the closer a line that works without it. A stranger on the blog must get the joke.
10. **The biggest thing in P4 is the joke.** The knee-deep paper in #13, the empty list held up in #14, the raised SEEN flag in #12. When the one visual event is a small thing in the background, the panel reads as talk.

## How soft drafts fail

- **The reader is ahead of Igor.** P1 shows the whole joke and P4 is Igor noticing it three panels later. A warm, plausible closer that costs nobody anything.
- **P3 says the punchline.** P3 sets up P4 so completely that P4 only demonstrates it, often with the same staging.
- **Repetition, not escalation.** P2 and P3 say the same thing twice; the closer explains itself in two sentences; Igor's P4 line is his P2 line again.
- **Domain punchline.** Funny only if you know what a git author, a token or a context window is.
- **A wisecrack about the mechanism** instead of the pile the reader is looking at.

The common thread: a premise is not a punchline. If P4 restates the premise,
demonstrates P3, or gives a reply Igor could reasonably accept, the strip is a
setup with no turn.

## Pre-flight checklist (run on every pitch before drawing)

Answer each in one line in the pitch file. A "no" on 1–5 means rewrite before drawing.

1. **The turn.** Say P4 in one sentence. Is it new information, or Igor noticing what the reader saw in P1?
2. **The giveaway.** Read P3 alone. Can you guess P4 from it? If yes, blunt P3 (give it a flat "DEAL." or a question with no hint).
3. **One beat.** Is Larry's closer one sentence, six words or fewer, with no "because"? If it needs a second sentence to be understood, the picture isn't doing its job.
4. **The bill.** Who pays in P4, and what exactly do they lose? Name it. "Nobody" means no joke.
5. **Wrong on purpose.** Would Igor be right to accept the P4 reply? If it's polite and plausible, it's soft. Larry must be literal, serene, and un-arguable.
6. **Setup pays off.** List every named prop and beat in P1–P3 (a bow, a sleeping robot, a clock). Each one pays off in P4 or gets cut.
7. **Escalation.** Does P3 make P2 bigger, or say it again?
8. **Picture change.** Is P4's picture different from P3's, and is the biggest thing in P4 the joke?
9. **No homework.** Can a stranger read it with no knowledge of tokens, git, models or the tools involved? If one term is needed, it goes on a prop, not in the closer.
10. **The brief's words.** Is there a verbatim quote in the brief? Use it as the straight line or the closer.
11. **Reading order.** Does the punchline balloon read last? Three balloons in a panel need the staircase rule.
12. **Privacy, line by line.** Every balloon: nothing about work, health, family, career or a journal confession. The AI machinery misbehaving is the only subject.
13. **Honour the ending.** Read every earlier line against P4. Does any line ask for the thing P4 throws away?

## The one-line test

Cover P1–P3. Read P4 alone. If it's funny on its own and funnier with the
setup, draw it. If it only makes sense with the setup, it's the setup.
